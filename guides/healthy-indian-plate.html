The Healthy Indian Plate: Balanced Meals Without Giving Up Your Favourite Foods

Build a healthy Indian plate using ICMR-NIN 2024 guidelines: portions, vegetarian protein, oil and salt limits, and sample North and South Indian meal plans.

By the Nirog editorial teamFact-checked against the sources listed belowUpdated 28 October 20268 min read

A healthy Indian plate is mostly vegetables, a moderate portion of whole grains or millets, a good serving of dal, beans, curd, eggs or other protein, and only a little oil, salt and sugar. You do not need to give up roti, rice, idli or your favourite sabzi. The change is mostly about proportions: more vegetables and pulses, fewer refined carbs and fried extras.

Key takeaways

  • The ICMR-NIN "My Plate for the Day" suggests vegetables, leafy greens, roots and tubers should make up about half of what you eat.
  • Cereals and millets should provide no more than about 45% of daily calories; most Indian diets exceed this.
  • Combining cereals and pulses (roughly 3 parts cereal to 1 part pulse) improves protein quality in vegetarian meals.
  • Keep cooking oil to roughly 25 to 30 g a day, salt under 5 g, and added sugar as low as possible.
  • Small swaps at home and when eating out add up more than strict diets you cannot sustain.

What the ICMR-NIN 2024 guidelines recommend

In 2024, the ICMR-National Institute of Nutrition (NIN) released revised Dietary Guidelines for Indians, with 17 guidelines covering all age groups. The guidelines note that more than half of India's total disease burden is linked to unhealthy diets.

My Plate for the Day

The model plate is based on a roughly 2,000 kcal day and draws from several food groups. Here is how the vegetarian version breaks down (raw weights per day):

Food group Amount per day Approx. share of energy
Cereals and millets 250 g 42%
Pulses (dal, beans, chana) 85 g 14%
Milk or curd 300 ml 11%
Vegetables and green leafy vegetables 400 g 9%
Fruits 100 g 3%
Nuts and seeds 35 g 9%
Fats and oils 27 g 12%

These amounts are a guide for an average adult, not a fixed prescription. Your needs depend on age, sex, body size, activity and health conditions. People who eat eggs, fish or chicken can swap part of the pulses for these foods.

Other important targets

  • Cereals and millets: no more than about 45% of total calories.
  • Pulses, beans and meat together: up to about 15% of calories.
  • Total fat: about 30% of calories or less.
  • Added sugar: under 5% of energy, roughly 20 to 25 g a day for adults.
  • Salt: WHO advises under 5 g a day (just under one level teaspoon) from all sources.

Getting portions right

You do not need a weighing scale every day. A plate method is easier:

  • Half the plate: vegetables and salad, such as sabzi, kosambari, raita with cucumber, or leafy greens.
  • A quarter: grains, such as two medium phulkas, a small bowl of rice, or two idlis.
  • A quarter: protein, such as a bowl of dal, rajma, chana, paneer, curd, eggs, fish or chicken.
  • On the side: a small bowl of curd or buttermilk, and a fruit for dessert or a snack.

If you are still hungry, add more vegetables or dal before reaching for extra rice or rotis.

Protein in vegetarian diets

Many Indian vegetarians fall short on protein, mainly because grains dominate the plate and dal portions are thin. Cereals and pulses complement each other, so the classic pairings, like dal-chawal, rajma-rice, idli-sambar and khichdi, make sense. The guidelines suggest roughly 3 parts cereal to 1 part pulse.

Ways to add protein without supplements:

  • Make dal thicker, or serve a second protein such as chana, sprouts or curd.
  • Add paneer, tofu, soya chunks or besan chilla to breakfast or lunch.
  • Use roasted chana, peanuts or a handful of nuts as snacks.
  • Include milk or curd every day.

The guidelines advise against protein powders for building muscle. Most people can meet their needs through food, and very high intakes have not been shown to add more benefit.

Cutting back on refined carbs

White rice, maida, and sugary drinks digest quickly and can push blood sugar up sharply, especially in people with prediabetes or diabetes. You do not need to cut out rice; adjust the type and amount.

  • Mix millets such as jowar, bajra, ragi or foxtail millet into rotis and dosas.
  • Choose whole wheat atta, brown or hand-pounded rice, or mix white rice with dal and vegetables.
  • Limit maida-based breads, biscuits, and bakery items.
  • Swap sweetened drinks and packaged juices for water, buttermilk, nimbu pani without much sugar, or coconut water.

Oil, salt and sugar: the hidden extras

Oil

The guidelines suggest about 25 to 30 g (roughly 5 to 6 teaspoons) of cooking oil a day per adult. Rotate oils, such as mustard, groundnut, sesame or rice bran, to get a mix of fats, and keep ghee and butter small. Avoid reusing oil for deep frying, and limit vanaspati and packaged foods that may contain trans fats.

Salt

Much of our salt comes from pickles, papad, namkeen, sauces, instant noodles and restaurant food. Taste before salting, use lemon, herbs and spices for flavour, and keep the pickle to a small spoonful.

Sugar

Tea and coffee with two spoons of sugar several times a day quickly exceed the limit. Reduce gradually, and save mithai for occasions rather than daily habits.

Sample day of meals

These are examples to adapt, not prescriptions. Adjust portions to your hunger and activity.

Meal North Indian style South Indian style
Early morning Water, a few soaked almonds or walnuts Water, a few groundnuts or soaked almonds
Breakfast Vegetable besan chilla or moong dal chilla with mint chutney; a glass of milk Two idlis or a ragi dosa with plenty of sambar and coconut chutney
Mid-morning A seasonal fruit such as guava or orange A seasonal fruit such as papaya or banana
Lunch Two multigrain rotis, dal, mixed vegetable sabzi, cucumber-onion salad, curd Small portion of rice mixed with millets, sambar or rasam, poriyal, kosambari, curd
Evening snack Roasted chana or sprouts chaat; tea with less sugar Sundal (boiled chana or green gram); filter coffee with less sugar
Dinner Jowar or wheat roti, palak paneer or rajma, a large bowl of salad Vegetable upma or pesarattu, avial or a vegetable kootu, buttermilk

Non-vegetarians can replace one pulse dish with eggs, fish or chicken cooked with little oil.

Eating out without losing your balance

  • Start with a salad, soup or tandoori vegetables before the main meal.
  • Choose dry, grilled or tandoori items over cream-based gravies and deep-fried starters.
  • Share rich dishes, and order plain roti or steamed rice instead of naan, butter naan or biryani every time.
  • Ask for less oil, salt or butter. Many kitchens will adjust.
  • Skip sugary drinks; choose water, plain lassi with no added sugar, or buttermilk.
  • For street food, pick options like sprouts chaat, idli, or roasted corn, and keep fried snacks occasional.

When to see a doctor

See a doctor or registered dietitian if you have diabetes, kidney disease, heart disease, high blood pressure, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or are planning major dietary changes. Also seek advice for:

  • Unexplained weight loss, loss of appetite or difficulty swallowing
  • Persistent tiredness, breathlessness or pale skin, which can suggest anaemia
  • Ongoing stomach pain, bloating, or changes in bowel habits
  • Very restrictive eating or fears around food

For severe symptoms like chest pain, sudden breathlessness or collapse, call 108 or 112.

Frequently asked questions

Is rice bad for health?

No. Rice can be part of a healthy diet when the portion is moderate and the plate also has plenty of vegetables and dal. Mixing in millets or choosing less polished rice adds fibre. People with diabetes should discuss portion sizes with their doctor or dietitian.

How much ghee is okay?

Ghee counts towards your total daily fat. A small amount, such as a teaspoon on dal or roti, can fit within the 25 to 30 g oil and fat range for most healthy adults. If you have high cholesterol or heart disease, ask your doctor about your limit.

Can vegetarians get enough protein without supplements?

Most can, by combining cereals with pulses, including milk or curd daily, and adding paneer, soya, nuts or seeds. The ICMR-NIN guidelines discourage protein powders for muscle building. Athletes and people with specific health needs can get personalised advice from a dietitian.

Are millets better than wheat or rice?

Millets are generally richer in fibre and some minerals, and they add welcome variety. They are not magic foods, though. Portion size, cooking method and what you eat alongside them still matter.

Is jaggery healthier than sugar?

Jaggery contains small amounts of minerals, but your body handles it much like sugar. It still counts as added sugar, so keep it limited.
